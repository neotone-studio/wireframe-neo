“I don’t lose inspiration while searching for another instrument”

Golsa Nazari on what switching instruments costs an idea, and what happens when the switch disappears.

Interview · 4 min · August 2026

Golsa Nazari is a handpan player, composer and teacher from Iran. She started at five, on piano, and found the handpan years later. It redirected her playing almost immediately. “It gave me a new way to express emotions that I couldn’t easily put into words.”

Her music is cinematic and emotional, built on Persian and Middle Eastern material and arranged with modern textures. Asked what feeds it, she lists life experiences, emotions, Persian culture, nature, and silence. “Sometimes a single feeling becomes an entire composition.”

The studio holds acoustic handpans, a piano, microphones and monitors, and the Neotone sits among them rather than in place of anything. She works both inside a DAW and away from one, sketching directly on the instrument before arranging. Reverb and delay for space, occasionally a looper, though she prefers to keep the sound close to natural.

What changed

What the instrument altered was less the sound than the interruption. Moving between scales used to mean moving between instruments, and an idea rarely survived the trip.

“I don’t lose inspiration while searching for another instrument. The creative flow stays uninterrupted.”

Asked for a moment she will not forget, she names that one: “the first time I realized I could move between completely different scales in seconds while keeping the same creative flow.”

It has since taken her somewhere she had not planned. She uses it as a compositional sketchbook, testing ideas quickly before committing them to acoustic instruments, and increasingly to write for larger forces. Her own description of that is the best line in the conversation: composing orchestral ideas while still thinking like a handpan player.

Advice

What she offers players considering one is a reframing rather than a recommendation. “Don’t think of it as a replacement for an acoustic handpan. Think of it as a completely new creative instrument that expands your possibilities.”

And once it arrives: “Don’t just play the presets. Explore different scales and create your own sounds. That’s where the instrument really becomes personal.”
